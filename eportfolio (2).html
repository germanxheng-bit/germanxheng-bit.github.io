Learning to Communicate with Purpose

Welcome to my ePortfolio — a curated record of the writing, analysis, and professional growth I have pursued throughout this Business Communication course. This site is both an archive and a demonstration: it shows not merely what I produced, but how each document reflects a deeper understanding of effective, audience-centered communication.

I came into this course as a Quantitative Economics student — someone who spends more time thinking about data pipelines and SQL schemas than drafting formal letters. I discovered quickly that the skills are not as separate as they seem. Every chart, every report, every professional message is an act of communication: you are always making choices about what to say, how to say it, and who you are saying it to. This course gave me a vocabulary and a framework for those choices.

The central lesson I am taking from this semester is the classical rhetorical triad — ethos, pathos, and logos. Ethos is the credibility I establish as a writer through precision, professionalism, and accuracy. Pathos is my connection to the reader's needs, values, and circumstances. Logos is the logical structure and evidence that make my argument persuasive. I now apply all three lenses to everything I write.

This portfolio is organized into five sections. The About Me page introduces who I am outside the classroom. Job Application Materials showcases my résumé, cover letter, and thank-you letter, along with a reflection on what I learned. The Group Project page presents our team's formal report to Target Corporation and reflects on collaborative writing. The Correspondence page features my revised business letter with a rhetorical reflection. Together, these pages tell a story of a student who arrived thinking communication was a formality, and leaves understanding it is a craft — one I intend to keep developing throughout my career.

Reflective Statement — Job Application Materials

Before this course, I approached job application materials as templates to fill in. I had a résumé that listed my projects and skills, and I assumed a cover letter was simply a longer version of the same list. What I learned this semester is that each of these documents serves a distinct rhetorical purpose — and understanding those purposes transformed how I write all three.

The résumé, I came to understand, is not a biography — it is an argument. Every bullet point should answer an implicit employer question: What did you accomplish, and why does it matter to me? Learning to write achievement-oriented bullets that quantify outcomes and use active language gave my résumé a specificity it previously lacked. My two data analytics projects — the Vineyard Sales SQL dashboard and the Wine Quality analysis — were the foundation, but translating them into résumé language that would resonate with an employer in the winemaking industry required deliberate audience awareness.

The cover letter for the Gallo Winery internship was the most meaningful writing challenge of the semester. Rather than summarizing my résumé, I learned to use the letter to tell a story — one that connected my background in the Central Valley to a genuine interest in Gallo's work, and that demonstrated, rather than merely claimed, my analytical skills. The opening paragraph in particular went through multiple revisions; I wanted it to establish ethos and pathos simultaneously, grounding my credentials in a personal narrative that felt authentic rather than formulaic.

The mock thank-you letter taught me a genre I had genuinely never considered. I learned that a post-interview letter is not mere courtesy — it is a strategic opportunity to reinforce your strongest qualifications, address any gaps from the interview, and demonstrate professionalism and follow-through. Writing it in the context of a simulated Gallo interview helped me understand how to tailor even a brief letter to the specific details of a conversation.

Together, these three documents represent my first real attempt at constructing a complete professional narrative — one that is audience-centered, evidence-based, and rhetorically deliberate. That approach, I believe, is the most transferable lesson from this semester.

Reflective Statement — Group Project

Writing a Long Business Message

The Group Project Report was the most structurally demanding document I have ever produced. A formal business report differs fundamentally from the short-form writing I was used to — it must sustain a coherent argument across many pages, use headings and subheadings to guide a reader who may not read linearly, and present data visually in ways that add clarity rather than noise. Learning to write an executive summary that stands alone — condensing the entire report into a page without losing the essential logic — was one of the most challenging and instructive exercises of the semester.

I also came to appreciate the difference between academic writing and professional writing in a new way. In academic papers, thoroughness is a virtue; in business reports, concision is. Every table, every paragraph, every section header had to justify its presence by serving the reader's decision-making needs. We were not writing to demonstrate knowledge — we were writing to help Tiffany Trombley decide which scooters to stock. Keeping that audience and purpose in focus throughout the drafting process required discipline, especially when the research uncovered details I found interesting but that ultimately did not serve the report's central argument.

Working as a Team

The collaborative process taught me skills that no individual writing project could have. Our group of five brought different strengths: some members excelled at research and synthesis, others at visual design, others at prose editing. Early in the project, inconsistencies in tone and formatting across sections written independently made the draft feel fragmented. We resolved this by agreeing on a shared style guide and assigning one team member to do a unifying editorial pass before submission — a professional workflow I had never used before.

More challenging, and ultimately more rewarding, was learning to give and receive feedback on one another's writing. My instinct was to protect the sections I had drafted. The process of accepting substantive edits — having a paragraph I was proud of restructured by a teammate — required me to prioritize the document's quality over my own attachment to particular phrasing. That shift in perspective is, I think, what separates a collaborative writer from someone who merely divides tasks.

The group project confirmed that effective business communication is rarely a solo act. The most consequential documents in any organization are produced by teams, revised through feedback, and refined in response to real audience needs. I am a more patient, more flexible, and more audience-aware writer because of this experience.

Reflective Statement — Ethos, Pathos & Logos in the Business Letter

Ethos: Establishing Credibility

Before this course, I understood ethos primarily as a matter of credentials — listing the right experiences to seem qualified. This semester gave me a more nuanced understanding. In a business letter, ethos is built through every detail: the precision of your formatting, the appropriateness of your tone, the accuracy of the information you provide, and your adherence to the conventions your reader expects. When I first drafted this letter, I made several errors that quietly undermined my credibility — informal phrasing, inconsistent spacing, and a greeting that was not formatted to professional standard. The revised version corrects these, and in doing so signals to the reader that I am someone who takes professional communication seriously. Ethos, I now understand, is not just claimed — it is demonstrated.

Pathos: Connecting with the Reader

Pathos in a business letter does not mean sentimentality — it means genuine attention to the reader's perspective, needs, and values. The original draft of this letter was writer-centered: it focused on what I wanted (the volunteer position) without adequately considering what the reader needed (confidence that I would be reliable, organized, and aligned with the organization's mission). The revised letter addresses the reader's likely concerns more directly. By structuring my availability in a clear, easy-to-scan table, I respected the reader's time. By framing my experience in terms of contribution — "what I can bring to your organization" — rather than personal gain, I demonstrated awareness of her priorities. This shift from writer-centered to reader-centered thinking is, I believe, the most practically important lesson I have internalized this semester.

Logos: Organizing a Logical Argument

The original letter was organized loosely — it moved from one point to the next without a clear logical structure. The revised version builds its case more deliberately: it opens with a clear statement of purpose and fit, provides specific evidence of relevant experience through the table, addresses the practical question of availability, and closes with a specific, professional call to action. The logic flows from introduction to evidence to conclusion in a way that mirrors how a decision-maker actually reads correspondence — looking first for relevance, then for evidence, then for next steps.

Reflecting on this letter through the lens of ethos, pathos, and logos has shown me that these three appeals are not techniques to add after a draft is written — they are the architecture of effective communication. Every choice, from word selection to paragraph structure to visual formatting, either builds or undermines the persuasive case you are making. Recognizing that architecture, and learning to build it deliberately, is the skill I will carry most clearly from this course into my professional life.
