Learning to Communicate

Welcome to my ePortfolio! This is my curated collection of writing, thinking and professional development I created throughout this Business Communication course. This site is an archive that demonstrates how my skills as a communicator and professional have grown over the term.

Effective business communication is far more than grammar and formatting. It is the art of meeting an audience where they are by understanding their needs, their concerns and their expectations. It's about crafting a message that is clear, credible, and compelling. This semester, I have learned that every document I create is a rhetorical argument: I am always making choices about ethos (my credibility as a writer), pathos (my connection to the reader's values and emotions), and logos (the logic and evidence that support my claims). These three classical appeals have become lenses through which I now read and revise all my professional writing.

This portfolio is organized into five sections. The About Me page introduces who I am beyond the classroom. The Job Application Materials page showcases my résumé, cover letter and thank-you letter, which I have refined through multiple rounds of feedback. The Group Project page presents our team's formal report and reflects on what collaborative writing taught me. The Correspondence page features a revised business letter that demonstrates how I applied instructor feedback to meet professional standards.

Together, these pages tell a story of growth. I arrived in this course believing that professional writing was mostly a matter of following templates. I now understand that communication is a practice. One that demands ongoing attention to purpose, audience, and context. I hope this portfolio demonstrates that understanding, and I invite you to explore each section to see the evidence for yourself.

Thank you for visiting. Whether you are a future employer, an instructor, or a fellow student, I hope what you find here reflects the genuine effort and reflection I have invested in becoming a stronger communicator.

Reflective Statement — Job Application Materials

Before this course, I thought a résumé was simply a list of degrees, jobs, and skills. What I learned this semester is that a résumé is a carefully engineered argument. Every bullet point must answer an unsaid question the employer is asking: Why should I hire you over the other candidates? Learning to write strong achievement-based bullet points, focusing on results rather than duties and quantifying outcomes wherever possible has transformed a document I had used for years into something genuinely persuasive.

The cover letter was perhaps my most significant writing challenge. I had always written generic cover letters that summarized my résumé rather than extending it. This semester, I have learned that a cover letter must create a narrative. It should explain not just what I have done, but why I want this specific role at this specific organization, and how my background positions me to solve the employer's particular problems. Revising my cover letter through multiple drafts, applying feedback about specificity and audience awareness, has allowed me to produce a document I am genuinely proud of.

The mock thank-you letter was a genre I had never written before. I learned that a thank-you letter is more than etiquette. It is a strategic continuation of the interview. It reminds the interviewer of the key strengths I bring, addresses any concerns that may have arisen during the conversation, and demonstrates professionalism and follow-through. These are all qualities employers value highly.

Taken together, these three documents represent a complete professional narrative. They demonstrate that I understand not only how to present my qualifications, but how to frame them for maximum impact with a specific audience. That skill of tailoring a message to the audience is, I believe, the most transferable lesson I am taking from this course.

Reflective Statement — Group Project

Writing a Long Business Message

The Group Project Report was the most structurally demanding document I have ever produced. A formal business report differs fundamentally from the short-form writing I was used to because it must sustain a coherent argument across many pages, use headings and subheadings to guide a reader who may not read linearly, and present data visually in ways that add clarity rather than noise. Learning to write an executive summary that stands alone by condensing the entire report into a page without losing the essential logic was one of the most challenging and instructive exercises of the semester.

I also came to appreciate the difference between academic writing and professional writing in a new way. In academic papers, thoroughness is a virtue; in business reports, concision is. Every table, every paragraph, every section header had to justify its presence by serving the reader's decision-making needs. Keeping the audience and purpose in focus throughout the drafting process required discipline, especially when the research uncovered details I found interesting but that ultimately did not serve the report's central argument.

Working as a Team

The collaborative process taught me skills that no individual writing project could have. Our group of five brought different strengths: some members excelled at research and synthesis, others at visual design, others at prose editing. Early in the project, inconsistencies in tone and formatting across sections written independently made the draft feel fragmented. We resolved this by agreeing on a shared style guide and assigning one team member to do a unifying editorial pass before submission. This was a great professional workflow technique I had never used before.

More challenging, and ultimately more rewarding, was learning to give and receive feedback on one another's writing. My instinct was to protect the sections I had drafted. The process of accepting substantive edits by my teammates required me to prioritize the document's quality over my own attachment to particular phrasing. That shift in perspective is, I think, what separates a collaborative writer from someone who merely divides tasks.

The group project confirmed that effective business communication is rarely a solo act. The most consequential documents in any organization are produced by teams, revised through feedback, and refined in response to real audience needs. I am a more patient, more flexible, and more audience-aware writer because of this experience.

Reflective Statement — Ethos, Pathos & Logos in the Business Letter

Ethos: Establishing Credibility

Before this course, I understood ethos primarily as a matter of credentials or listing the right experiences to seem qualified. This semester gave me a more nuanced understanding. In a business letter, ethos is built through every detail: the precision of your formatting, the appropriateness of your tone, the accuracy of the information you provide, and your adherence to the conventions your reader expects. When I first drafted this letter, I made several errors that quietly undermined my credibility, such as informal phrasing, inconsistent spacing, and a greeting that was not formatted to a professional standard. The revised version corrects these, and in doing so signals to the reader that I am someone who takes professional communication seriously.

Pathos: Connecting with the Reader

Pathos in a business letter does not just mean sentimentality; it means genuine attention to the reader's perspective, needs, and values. The original draft of this letter was writer-centered because it focused on what I wanted (the volunteer position) without adequately considering what the reader needed (confidence that I would be reliable, organized, and aligned with the organization's mission). The revised letter addresses the reader's likely concerns more directly. By framing my experience in terms of contribution, "what I can bring to your organization", I demonstrated awareness of her priorities. This shift from writer-centered to reader-centered thinking is, I believe, one of the most practically important lessons I have internalized this semester.

Logos: Organizing a Logical Argument

The original letter was also organized loosely; it moved from one point to the next without a clear logical structure. The revised version builds its case more deliberately: it opens with a clear statement of purpose and fit, provides specific evidence of relevant experience through the table, addresses the practical question of availability, and closes with a specific, professional call to action. The logic flows from introduction to evidence to conclusion in a way that mirrors how a decision-maker actually reads correspondence.

Reflecting on this letter through the lens of ethos, pathos, and logos has shown me that these three appeals are the architecture of effective communication. Every choice, from word selection to paragraph structure to visual formatting, either builds or undermines the persuasive case you are making. Recognizing that architecture, and learning to build it deliberately, is the skill I will carry most clearly from this course into my professional life.
